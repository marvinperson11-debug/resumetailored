How do you tailor a resume to a job description?

Follow 7 steps: get the full posting, pull its priority keywords, match each one to real experience, rewrite your bullets in the posting's language, reorder for relevance, fix the formatting, then check your score before sending. Each is detailed below.

1. Get the full job posting, not just the title

Copy the entire description — responsibilities, required qualifications, and preferred qualifications. The required/preferred split matters: required items are usually what a screening filter checks first.

Save the posting somewhere you can find it again. Postings expire or get edited, and you will want the exact wording when you check your work. Note the posting date and any salary range, and pay attention to how the posting phrases requirements: "required" and "must have" are what a screening filter checks first; "preferred", "a plus", and "bonus" are tie-breakers, not deal-breakers.

2. Pull out the posting's priority keywords

List the specific skills, tools, certifications, and job titles the posting repeats or lists under "requirements." These are the terms most likely to be checked against your resume — see how ATS screening works for why exact wording matters.

Count repeats: a skill named three times matters more than one named once, and the job title itself is a keyword. Aim for 8 to 12 priority terms, not fifty. Keep them in a short list next to your resume while you work, so you can check each one off as it lands in a bullet.

3. Match each keyword to real experience — or don't use it

For every keyword on your list, ask whether you've actually done that work. If yes, note which job and which result. If no, leave it out. This step is the one that keeps tailoring honest instead of just keyword-stuffing.

Write the matching job and result next to each keyword. If a keyword matches nothing you have done, leave it out — and take it as a signal: if more than a third of the required items don't match anything real, the role may be a stretch, and no amount of rewriting fixes that. Never relabel a past job title to match the posting's title; a background check reads the real one.

4. Rewrite your existing bullets in the posting's language

Where you have a genuine match, rewrite the bullet using the posting's own term instead of your own internal title for the same thing (e.g., if you did what the posting calls "account management" but you called it "client relationship work," use the posting's phrase — only if it's an accurate description of what you did).

Keep each bullet to one claim with its result attached, and keep numbers exact — never round a result up to match the posting. Swap the verb to the posting's verb only when it describes the same work you actually did. If the posting says "led" and you supported, write "supported"; the honest verb still matches the keyword.

5. Reorder for relevance

Put your most relevant experience and skills nearer the top of each section. An ATS and a human reviewer both tend to weight what comes first.

The top third of page one is what gets read first, so move your most relevant role's bullets up and let the skills section follow the posting's order. Move education or certifications up only if the posting emphasizes them. Everything else can stay where it is — tailoring is reordering, not rewriting your whole history.

6. Check the formatting is ATS-readable

Single column, standard section headings, no tables or text boxes, a common font. Formatting that looks fine on screen can still parse badly — see the parsing section of how ATS screening works.

Use standard section headings — Experience, Skills, Education — and keep the layout to one column with no tables or text boxes. Most applicant systems skip headers and footers, so don't put contact details or keywords there. Save the file the way the posting asks, usually .docx or PDF, then run it through the free ATS checker to confirm it parses cleanly.

7. Check your match before you send it

Run the tailored resume and the job posting through an ATS checker to see which required terms are still missing, then decide — truthfully — whether you can address any of them.

The checker returns the terms still missing. For each one, ask whether you actually did that work. If yes, find the bullet where it belongs and add the posting's term. If no, leave it out. A match score is a gap list, not a grade — a resume at 80% with every claim true beats one at 97% with two invented ones.

What does a tailored resume bullet look like, before and after?

Below is a fictional, illustrative example — not a real candidate, employer, or generated output. The "after" bullet reuses only what the "before" bullet already says, rewritten in the posting's own terms; nothing new is added.

Illustrative example Job posting (excerpt)
"We're looking for a Customer Success Manager who can manage a portfolio of client accounts, reduce churn, and lead quarterly business reviews using CRM data."
Before
"Managed client relationships and helped improve retention for an assigned portfolio of accounts. Used CRM software to track renewals and presented updates to clients each quarter."
After
"Customer Success Manager overseeing a portfolio of client accounts; used CRM data to track renewals and reduce churn, and led quarterly business reviews with clients."

Every fact in "After" — the portfolio of accounts, the CRM tool use, the quarterly client presentations, the retention/churn outcome — already appears in "Before." Nothing is added: "churn" and "quarterly business reviews" are just the posting's own words for retention and the quarterly updates the candidate already did. No new employer, number, or skill was introduced.

Same rule, different role. Here is a warehouse example.

Illustrative example Job posting (excerpt)
"Looking for a Warehouse Associate who can pick and pack 150 orders per shift, operate a forklift, and keep inventory counts accurate in our WMS."
Before
"Picked and packed 150 orders per shift in a busy warehouse. Operated a forklift to move pallets and updated inventory counts in the warehouse management system."
After
"Warehouse Associate picking and packing 150 orders per shift; operated a forklift daily and kept inventory counts accurate in the WMS."

Every fact in "After" — the 150 orders, the forklift, the inventory counts — already appears in "Before." Only the posting's own words were adopted: the job title "Warehouse Associate", the abbreviation "WMS" for the warehouse management system the candidate already used, and "accurate" for the counts they already kept.

What's the checklist before you submit a tailored resume?

  • Copied the full job posting, not just the title
  • Listed the specific skills, tools, and titles the posting repeats
  • Matched each keyword to real experience — and left out the ones that don't apply
  • Rewrote existing bullets in the posting's own language, where accurate
  • Reordered sections so the most relevant experience comes first
  • Checked formatting is single-column and ATS-readable
  • Ran it through an ATS checker and reviewed what's still missing
  • Proofread for accuracy — nothing added that isn't true

What tailoring does not do

Tailoring rewrites what's already true in the posting's language. It does not add skills, roles, or results you don't have, and it does not turn a stretch role into a match. If the posting needs a certification you don't hold or three years of experience you don't have, the honest answer is to skip it or apply knowing the gap, not to write the missing pieces in.

It also doesn't fix a resume with no relevant experience at all. Tailoring works when the experience is real and the words are wrong. When the experience itself is missing, the fix is a different role, a course, or a project — not a rewrite.

How to tailor your summary and skills section

Your summary is two lines at the top, and it should echo the posting's core need in your own true words — the role, the setting, and the one result that matters most. Don't copy the posting's summary sentence; write yours so a human believes it.

Order your skills list to match the posting's order, and drop skills the posting doesn't ask for toward the bottom. Never list a tool you haven't used: a skills line is a claim, and an interviewer will test it.

Mistakes that hurt a tailored resume

The most common ones are easy to avoid once you know them. Copying the posting's sentences word for word reads as copied, and a human spots it in seconds. Hiding keywords in white text or a tiny footer is keyword stuffing, and applicant systems flag it. Changing a past job title to match the posting's title breaks background checks. And the quietest mistake: tailoring once, then sending the same version everywhere — a tailored resume is per posting, every time.

Proofread last. A tailored resume that still carries the last company's name from a previous application tells the reader exactly how much care went into it.

How long does it take to tailor a resume?

By hand, 30 to 45 minutes per application the first few times, faster once the process is familiar. The slow part is the matching, not the writing. With an AI resume tailor, the rewrite itself takes under 30 seconds — but you still need about five minutes to check every claim against what you actually did, because the check is the part that keeps it honest.

Do you tailor for every job you apply to?

Yes, if the job is one you actually want. A tailored resume takes more time than a generic one, so spend it where it counts: roles that fit your real experience and that you'd take if offered. For roles that are a stretch on paper, a quick pass on the summary and skills is enough to test the waters — but don't send a fully tailored resume to a job you wouldn't accept.

Keep a master resume with everything on it, and tailor a copy per posting. Never overwrite the master. That way each application starts from the full record, and nothing gets lost between versions.
